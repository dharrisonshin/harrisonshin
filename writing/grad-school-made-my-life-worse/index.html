Grad School Made My Life Worse

nov 9, 2026

My grad school experience has absolutely sucked.

It has been a colossal waste of my time, energy, and resources. I would even say it has made my life worse. It put ideas in my head that made me doubt myself, held me back, and pushed me toward terrible career moves that brought me a year of suffering and financial hardship.

Before I started my master’s program in fall 2024, I had a pretty good job. It was part-time, 20–24 hours a week, but it paid well. It was a role some of my classmates hoped to get after earning their master’s degrees.

I already had it.

During a class discussion, I brought up some frustrations with that job. Overall, the job was mostly good. Instead of helping me think through how to advocate for myself in the role I had, I was told to just leave.

When the instructor learned that I had been a student at the institution where I worked and that this was my only professional experience in higher education, they became even more insistent that I needed another job.

“I need to see you in another role before the end of the year.”

Looking back, that bothers me. Why was leaving the answer? Why wasn’t there more consideration of what I already had, what was working, or whether I could address my frustrations without walking away from a mostly good job?

I’ll admit I didn’t have to take the advice. I made my own decisions, and I’m living with them. But these were supposedly well-regarded people in the field. At the time, I believed they understood my situation and knew what they were talking about.

My mistake was treating generic career advice as if it had been carefully considered for me. Taking responsibility for following it doesn’t mean I have to pretend it was good advice.

In hindsight, I should have stayed at that job, stayed out of grad school, and focused on growing my network and experience.

The coursework hasn’t made me feel much better about that decision. Theories are supposed to help us understand things. I often found them restrictive. Instead of helping me think more openly, they felt like boxes I was expected to think inside. I didn’t need more prescribed ways to interpret people and situations.

Then there are the buzzwords. Everyone loves to say “meet the students where they’re at.” But when I ask people what they actually mean by that, I don’t get a clear example. If we can’t explain what it looks like in practice, what are we actually saying?

I’ve also seen people take credit for students’ success when they sometimes had nothing to do with it. That bothers me. Supporting students matters, but so does recognizing when their accomplishments are theirs, not evidence of how great we are at our jobs.

That’s the disconnect I’ve found so frustrating: what gets taught and talked about in higher education versus what actually gets practiced. Too much of it has felt shallow and performative.

None of this means I stopped caring about the work I was doing or the students I was supporting. I still care about both. I just didn’t need these theories or the constant repetition of phrases like “meet the students where they’re at” to care about them.

Now I’m deep in debt: student loans, personal loans, and credit cards. Grad school has been an enormous time and money suck, and I’m six months away from graduating. From here on out, I’m doing the bare minimum academically to finish and get the degree. That is not the same as doing the bare minimum for students.

I now see this master’s primarily as a credential I need to advance my career, make more money, and hopefully get into positions where I can make the changes I want to see. That’s why I’m finishing. It doesn’t mean I think the experience has been worth what it cost me.

I’m not worried about colleagues, professors, or potential employers reading this. One thing I learned that I am taking to heart is to be my authentic self. That includes criticizing what I see as shortcomings in higher education and telling my own story honestly. If someone wants to shun me or not hire me for expressing those opinions, then I don’t need to associate myself with them.

I still want to do meaningful work and support students. I’m finishing this degree because I hope it helps me do more of that, not because it changed my mind about what I’ve experienced.

Caring about the work doesn’t mean I have to stay quiet about what I think is wrong with it.
