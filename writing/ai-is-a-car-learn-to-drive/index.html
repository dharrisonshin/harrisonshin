AI Is a Car. Learn to Drive.

nov 12, 2026

tech

In August 1888, Bertha Benz took her husband's car without telling him, loaded up their two teenage sons, and drove about 65 miles to her mother's house. It was the first long-distance car trip in history.

Visiting her mom was the cover story. The real reason was the car. Carl Benz had built the Benz Patent-Motorwagen and shown it in public two years earlier, and almost nobody cared. People were skeptical of the "horseless carriage" and didn't see the point. Carl was starting to doubt it himself. So Bertha set out to prove to him, and to everyone else, that it actually worked.

It wasn't a smooth ride. She patched the car along the way with a hat pin, a garter, and a cobbler's leather, and her sons pushed it up the hills. She didn't even have a license. Carl had received the world's first one just days earlier.

It did. The trip showed people the car was useful, and Benz & Cie. grew into the world's largest car factory of its day. Carl later said, "She was more daring than I."

Nobody went back to horses. We didn't ban the car because it was strange, unreliable, or occasionally dangerous. We built roads, wrote traffic laws, and taught people to drive. The car didn't go away. We learned to use it well.

I think about that a lot when people tell me AI is going to ruin us.

My AI reads my email before I do. It triages my inbox, manages my calendar, and only pings me when something actually needs me. I run a small team of AI assistants. I built this website with AI coding agents. So yeah, I have opinions about AI.

Here's the one I keep coming back to: AI is a tool, like a car. Some people drive responsibly, ethically, and productively. Some people text at 80 mph. We don't ban cars because of bad drivers.

And if you don't want to use AI at all? That's fine. Plenty of people choose not to drive, and nobody thinks less of them. I just don't think it's fair to tell everyone else to walk.

I keep hearing the same six arguments against AI. I take them seriously, and some of them are partly right. Here's why they're wrong.

Before and after

Before: the default setup. Notifications on, every ping pulling you out of whatever you're doing. Microsoft's own telemetry says the average worker it tracks gets 117 emails a day, and the default is reading, sorting, and answering every one of them by hand, in between everything else.

After: my notifications are off. AI reads, summarizes, drafts, deletes, and archives. My assistants handle the inbox and the calendar, and they only flag what actually needs me. The time I used to spend sorting goes to building. That's how my website and projects exist at all.

I'm not going to pretend I measured the hours. But try this: count how many emails you opened this week that didn't need you. That's the time AI gives back.

1. Thinking

The argument: people who "think" with AI are skipping the hard, beautiful work of living with an idea until it changes them. Why think when you can just prompt?

I'll concede part of it: some people DO use AI to do their thinking for them. In a small, not-yet-peer-reviewed MIT Media Lab study of 54 people, those who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed the weakest brain connectivity of any group and struggled to quote their own essays. A Microsoft Research survey of 319 knowledge workers found that the more people trusted AI, the less critical thinking they reported doing.

But that isn't everyone who uses AI. It's the bad drivers. And the research says it comes down to how you drive. In a PNAS field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students, kids with plain GPT-4 did 48% better on practice problems. Then, without the AI, they scored 17% worse on the exam than kids who never had it, because they'd used it as a crutch. A version that gave hints instead of answers largely erased that drop. Same engine, different driving. And in a Harvard randomized trial in an intro physics course, students with a well-designed AI tutor posted learning gains more than double those of students in an active-learning class, in less time.

I argue with my AI. I make it poke holes in my takes. The question isn't "why think when I can prompt?" It's what you do with what comes back.

2. Artists

The argument: AI is built on artists' work without consent, and it profits off the very things that make us human.

The consent part is real. A court just approved Anthropic paying authors $1.5 billion, about $3,000 per book, for downloading pirated books. Creators deserve consent, credit, and pay.

But look at what that case actually shows: the system pushing back, and drawing the line in a pretty smart place. In the same case, the judge ruled that training on books was "exceedingly transformative" and fair use, but that downloading pirated copies to build a library was not. Learning from work is one thing. Stealing it is another. We already draw that line for people. You can read every book in the library and let it shape how you write. You can't shoplift the books.

And the pay is starting to show up. In 2023, the AP licensed part of its text archive to OpenAI and said it "firmly supports a framework that will ensure intellectual property is protected and content creators are fairly compensated for their work." Opt-outs exist too. Google lets any website use a robots.txt token called Google-Extended to keep its content out of training future Gemini models, and it doesn't affect search ranking. Is any of this perfect? No. But it's not "AI takes everything and nobody can do anything" either.

The U.S. Copyright Office says AI output can only be copyrighted where a human determined the expressive elements, and prompts alone don't count. The law is on team human. So am I.

Almost every creative industry profits off humans: publishers off writers, labels off musicians. The question is whether the humans get a fair deal. That's a fight about contracts and courts, not about whether the tool should exist.

And a lot of what AI does is help people, not replace them. In a study of 5,179 support agents, AI raised productivity 14% on average and 34% for novices. It helped the people who needed help most. And artists are picking it up by choice: MoMA acquired Refik Anadol's Unsupervised, a work that uses generative AI to interpret and transform more than 200 years of art from MoMA's collection. That's a human artist with a new brush, and one of the most important museums in the world hanging the result.

Anti-human would be replacing people and calling it progress. What I mostly see is people getting a better tool.

3. Creativity

The argument: art is an embodied offering of self. Machines have no bodies, so what they make is slop.

I get it. But plenty of human artists make slop. Stuff not even worthy of being on your mom's refrigerator. And there's way more of it than good art. Being made by a human doesn't automatically make something good. And AI can make beautiful art.

For me it's personal. I wanted to make things: a website, a video game. Without AI, I never could have. With how long it would've taken to learn those skills, I might never have tried. AI gave me the tools to carry out my vision. The vision, the taste, and the intent are still mine. AI just lowered the barrier to making them real.

Hideo Kojima, the game designer behind Metal Gear Solid and Death Stranding, has an X (formerly Twitter) bio that reads "Game Creator: 70% of my body is made of movies." That's my dream: taste built from everything you've watched, played, and lived. That's the embodied part. No tool replaces it.

In 1859, Baudelaire called photography "the refuge of every would-be painter, every painter too ill-endowed or too lazy to complete his studies." Now it hangs in museums, and cameras put image-making in everyone's hands. In a Science Advances experiment, AI story ideas made writers' stories more creative and better written, especially for the less creative writers. The catch was that the stories got more alike. So bring your own taste.

None of this is a knock on working artists. Their consent and pay matter (see above). More people making things is good.

4. Environment

The argument: AI devours electricity and water and treats the earth like something to strip-mine instead of steward.

I'm not going to pretend this one is made up. The local impacts are real. The IEA says data centers used about 1.5% of the world's electricity in 2024 and are projected to more than double to around 945 TWh by 2030. A typical AI-focused data center uses as much electricity as 100,000 households, and the biggest ones under construction will use 20 times that. Lawrence Berkeley National Lab estimated that U.S. data centers used about 17 billion gallons of water directly in 2023, and roughly 12 times that indirectly through the power plants feeding them. If one of those gets built next to your town, you deserve to know exactly what it's going to pull from the grid and the river.

But context matters. The same IEA report says data centers are about one-tenth of global electricity demand growth through 2030, less than industrial motors, air conditioning, or electric vehicles. Nobody's calling to ban AC. Google reports a median Gemini text prompt uses 0.24 Wh and about five drops of water, which it compares to watching TV for less than nine seconds. And it's getting better fast: Google says the energy per median prompt dropped 33x over a recent 12-month period. Those are the company's own numbers and haven't been independently verified, so take them with some salt. But that's not a monster.

Here's the part that gets left out: AI is also a real tool for using less.

  • Cooling. Back in 2016, DeepMind's machine learning cut the energy used to cool Google's data centers by up to 40%.
  • Flying. Contrails are a big chunk of aviation's warming. In a test with American Airlines, pilots using Google's AI predictions on 70 flights cut contrails by 54%. Small test, but a real proof point.
  • Batteries. Microsoft and Pacific Northwest National Laboratory used AI to screen more than 32 million materials and found a battery electrolyte that could use about 70% less lithium. It still needs more validation, but they built a working prototype in under nine months.
  • The big picture. The IEA estimates that broad use of existing AI tools could cut emissions by about 5% of energy-related emissions in 2035, which it says could be far larger than data centers' own emissions.

This is where the car comes back. Cars were filthy. We didn't ban them. In 1970, the Clean Air Act demanded a 90% cut in emissions from new cars by 1975. By 1975, first-generation catalytic converters and unleaded gas showed up, along with the first federal fuel economy standards. Today we have EVs. We kept the car and made it cleaner.

That's what stewardship looks like to me. Not refusing to build. Building well: cleaner power, smarter cooling, honest reporting, and holding companies to it.

5. Efficiency

The argument: efficiency isn't a virtue. Wisdom is a slow process of becoming, and AI is the easy way out.

Fair: efficiency for its own sake isn't a virtue. But in the parable of the talents, the servants who put their gifts to work are praised, and the one who buried his out of fear gets rebuked. Using a gift well isn't the easy way out. Burying it is.

Which brings me to Ronny Chieng. At Harvard's Class Day in May, he told grads their mission was to "destroy AI," then went after people who brag that AI can read their email, summarize it, and draft a response: "You can't do that? How useless are you?"

That's such a superficial jab. People don't have AI handle their email because they CAN'T do it. They do it because automating tedious, time-consuming tasks gives them time back for the things that actually matter. You want to sort through 117 emails a day by hand? Be my guest. But calling people useless for using time-saving tools? Get out of here. It's like saying "you use a bidet? you can't wipe your own ass? how useless are you??" Nobody calls you lazy for using a dishwasher.

To be fair, Chieng also said "the creating is the fun part," and I agree. That's exactly why I hand off the inbox and keep the creating.

6. Who's in charge

The argument: the printing press and the camera extended our hands and eyes. AI is different. It does cognitive work: writing, judging, deciding. And more and more, it acts on its own. Calling it "just a tool" is a category error.

This is the strongest argument of the six, and I'll be honest: AI is more general than a camera or a press. A camera does one thing. AI can draft the email, plan the week, and write the code. That's new.

But handing cognitive work to tools isn't. Calculators do our arithmetic. Spreadsheets run the numbers for entire companies. GPS picks our route. Nobody thinks the spreadsheet is the CFO.

We've panicked about this before. A lot. In Plato's Phaedrus, writing is accused of creating "forgetfulness in the learners' souls." In the 1490s, an abbot wrote In Praise of Scribes to defend hand-copying against the press, and then had it printed. In 1986, math teachers picketed over calculators with signs reading "The Button's Nothin' 'Til the Brain's Trained." Each one ended up extending us.

If a car is the everyday version, the extreme version is in the cockpit. Autopilot doesn't just help. It flies. A 2016 DOT Inspector General report says the FAA estimates automation is used 90% of the time in flight. And yet the FAA's rule is blunt: the pilot in command "is directly responsible for, and is the final authority as to, the operation of that aircraft." The FAA even warns pilots not to lean on it too hard. Autoflight systems "are useful tools for pilots and have improved safety," but continuous use "could lead to degradation of the pilot's ability to quickly recover the aircraft from an undesired state," so the FAA tells airlines to make sure pilots keep hand-flying. Sound familiar? Same lesson as the math students in section 1.

That's how I use AI. AI coding agents wrote the code for my website. They didn't decide what the site should be, what it says, or whether it was good enough to go live. I did. I'm the pilot in command.

A tool can take over a task. It can't take over where you're headed. Not unless you hand it the controls and walk out of the cockpit.

The quick ones

Jobs. A real concern. A Stanford working paper found a 13% relative employment drop for 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed jobs. I work in higher ed, so that's on us: we have to train students to drive the tool, not get replaced by it.

Hallucinations. Two lawyers and their firm were fined $5,000 for filing fake cases ChatGPT made up, then standing by them. But ChatGPT didn't sign that brief. A lawyer did, without reading the cases.

Cheating. Pew found 26% of teens have used ChatGPT for schoolwork, but only 18% think it's OK for writing essays. Our job is to build tools that coach instead of doing the work for them.

Power. The Stanford AI Index found the gap between open-weight and closed models shrank from 8% to 1.7% on some benchmarks in a year, and models like DeepSeek-R1 ship their weights under an MIT license. Concentration is worth watching, but the moat is shrinking.

Real lives

It can't be stopped because it's already making real lives better, and people don't give up things that help them. A few examples:

  • Science. AlphaFold predicted the structures of virtually all 200 million known proteins, is used by over two million people, and won its creators the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
  • Cancer screening. In Sweden's MASAI trial, AI-supported mammography found 29% more cancers, cut radiologists' reading workload by 44%, and saw 12% fewer cancers slip through between screenings (82 vs. 93).
  • Antibiotics. MIT researchers used AI to find halicin, which killed bacteria resistant to every known antibiotic in lab tests and cleared infections in mice. It's not a human drug yet.
  • Accessibility. Be My Eyes uses GPT-4 so blind and low-vision users can snap a photo and get a real description back. It'll even suggest what to cook with what's in your fridge.
  • Weather. DeepMind's GenCast beat the world's top ensemble forecast, ECMWF's ENS, on 97.2% of targets in 2019 tests, and each 15-day forecast takes 8 minutes on a single chip.
  • Everyday work. U.S. workers report generative AI saves them time equal to 1.6% of all work hours. That's my inbox, multiplied across the country.

54.6% of U.S. adults ages 18 to 64 used generative AI as of August 2025, way ahead of PCs (19.7%) and the internet (30.1%) three years after their launches. 78% of organizations reported using AI in 2024. Open weights can't be un-released. And it's global: if one country stops, the rest keep going.

So the question isn't whether. It's how. Nobody who gets a bidet ever goes back.

The challenge

Try these for one week:

  • Think first, then prompt. Write your own take before you ask for one.
  • Verify everything. If your name is on it, you own it.
  • Hand off one tedious thing. Your inbox, your calendar, your notes. Keep the creating for yourself.
  • Make it argue with you. Ask it to find the holes in your idea, not to agree with you.
  • Respect creators. Credit, license, and pay the people whose work you build on.

Then decide for yourself. Drive carefully, both hands on the wheel. And yes, get the bidet.
